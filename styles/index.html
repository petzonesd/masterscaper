Styles

Every layout is chasing a style — most people just haven't named it yet.

Style isn't decoration. It's a set of rules about hardscape, planting density, and negative space that tells you what to do next when you're not sure. Pick one before you buy a single stone.

[Photo: clean Iwagumi layout, odd number of stones, low carpet plant, minimal fish — pull from Hakkai gallery or Pet Zone aquascape display tanks]

Iwagumi

Japanese "rock formation." Three to five stones (always odd), one dominant, a single carpeting plant, and almost nothing else. The hardest style to do well because there's nowhere to hide a mistake.

[Photo: Dutch-style layout, dense plant rows/terraces, no visible hardscape, strong color contrast between species]

Dutch

No hardscape at all — the plants are the entire composition. Built in "streets" of contrasting color, leaf shape, and height. The oldest planted-tank style, older than the word "aquascaping" itself.

[Photo: jungle/nature-style tank, driftwood branching through dense planting, moss, natural chaos]

Jungle / Nature

The Amano-inspired style most people picture when they hear "aquascape." Driftwood and stone form a skeleton; plants grow in to soften and half-obscure it, aiming for a scene that looks found, not built.

[Photo: biotope tank, region-accurate hardscape and species — e.g. Amazon blackwater with leaf litter, or Southeast Asian stream tank]

Biotope

Recreates one specific real-world habitat — down to the region, water chemistry, and species list. Less about beauty by design, more about accuracy; the beauty is a side effect of getting it right.

Paludarium

Half aquarium, half terrarium. Land and water share the same tank, usually built around a waterfall or slope. More building project than aquascape — hardscape has to work wet and dry.

Iwagumi

Three stones minimum, always an odd count. One stone — the Oyaishi, or "parent stone" — is largest and set off-center, usually on the golden-ratio third. The others are smaller and echo its angle rather than compete with it.

Planting is almost always a single carpet species (dwarf hairgrass, Monte Carlo, or glosso) kept low and dense, so the stones stay the entire visual event. Fish are chosen for schooling motion, not color — a shoal of small tetras or rasboras reads as movement across the empty space, not a focal point.

Where it goes wrong: using an even number of stones, sizing them too similarly, or over-planting so the hardscape gets buried. Iwagumi lives or dies on restraint.
[Photo: overhead or front-on shot of stone placement mid-build, showing the Oyaishi positioning — from a Hakkai class setup if available]

Dutch

Built in "streets" — straight or gently curved rows of a single plant species, each row contrasting the next in leaf texture, color, and height. No hardscape, no negative space; the tank is planted edge to edge.

The skill is entirely in plant selection and trimming discipline — keeping each street a consistent height and shape so the rows read as bands from the front glass, not a jumble.

Where it goes wrong: letting one fast grower overrun its row, or choosing too many species — Dutch scapes work because of repetition, not variety.
[Photo: front-glass shot showing clear plant "streets" / color banding]

Jungle / Nature style

Driftwood or stone forms the structural skeleton first — branches, ridgelines, a sense of a place that existed before the plants arrived. Planting then grows in around and through that structure rather than replacing it.

Depth is the goal: foreground, midground, and background planted at different heights and leaf sizes to fake distance in a glass box that's only two feet deep.

Where it goes wrong: hardscape that looks assembled rather than found — branches that don't share a common "grain," or driftwood placed dead-center instead of off-axis.
[Photo: layout showing driftwood branching with moss/plants growing through it — this is likely your strongest existing photo category from Hakkai]

Biotope

Everything in the tank — substrate, hardscape, water parameters, and every plant and fish species — matches one real, specific location. Not "South American," but a particular river bend.

The visual result is often sparser and more muted than a jungle-style tank, because nature usually is. That's the point: accuracy is the metric, not drama.

Where it goes wrong: mixing species that are geographically close but not actually cohabitants — the most common shortcut that breaks the accuracy the style is built on.
[Photo: leaf-litter blackwater biotope or similar region-accurate setup]

Paludarium

Land and water in the same enclosure, usually connected by a slope, waterfall, or emersed planting that bridges the waterline. Requires hardscape and sealant choices that survive both wet and dry conditions.

Best treated as a build project first, aquascape second — plumbing a waterfall or misting system takes precedence over layout theory in the early stages.

Where it goes wrong: underestimating humidity and drainage — mold and rot on the land side are the most common paludarium failures, not anything aquatic.
[Photo: paludarium build showing waterline transition and emersed planting]
