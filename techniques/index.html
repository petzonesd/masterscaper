Techniques

Style tells you what you're building. This is how.

Four things determine whether a layout works: what goes in first, how it's planted, how it's maintained, and whether the plants can actually grow under your light and CO2. In that order.

Aquascaping technique comes down to four things, always in this order: hardscape placed dry before anything else, planting back-to-front and tall-to-short, a weekly trim and maintenance routine, and CO2 scaled to match your lighting. Skip the order, or let CO2 lag behind light, and a layout either never comes together or declines within a few months.

1. Hardscape placement

Hardscape goes in dry, before substrate is finalized and long before any water. Lay every stone or branch out on a table first — you should be able to walk away and come back without wanting to change anything before it goes in the tank.

  1. Establish a focal point off-center. Use the rule of thirds — never dead-center, never perfectly symmetrical unless the style calls for it (Dutch has no hardscape at all).
  2. Work with one material's natural lines. Mixing stone types or wood species that don't share a grain, color, or erosion pattern is the single most common beginner tell.
  3. Bury a third of what you think you need. Stones and roots that look "placed" usually aren't buried enough — nature doesn't leave things sitting on top of the substrate.
  4. Slope substrate back to front. A rear-high slope (using aquasoil or a substrate support) fakes depth and gives planting room to layer.

2. Planting order

Plant back to front, tall to short, so you're never reaching over something you just placed. Rhizome plants (anubias, bucephalandra, java fern) get tied or glued to hardscape — never buried, or the rhizome rots.

  1. Background stems and tall grasses first.
  2. Midground rhizomes and ferns tied to hardscape.
  3. Foreground carpet or low-growing species last, closest to the glass.
  4. Fill water slowly over a plate or plastic bag laid on the substrate so the flow doesn't uproot fresh plantings.
Carpet plants specifically (hairgrass, Monte Carlo, glosso) do better planted in small clumps with gaps than one dense mat — they fill in faster from many small starts than one struggling large one. For a carpet that's slow to take even planted this way, growing it emersed first with the dry start method lets roots establish before the tank is ever flooded.

3. Trim & maintenance schedule

A layout that peaks once and declines wasn't maintained — it was assembled. Weekly maintenance is what separates a photo-op from an aquascape that matures.

  • Weekly: water change (30–50%), glass cleaning, dose ferts per your plant load.
  • Every 1–2 weeks: trim stem plants back to just above the previous trim line — never let them reach the surface and flop.
  • As needed: thin carpet plants that are overgrowing their intended footprint; remove any melting or decaying leaves immediately, they foul water fast.
  • Monthly: check CO2 diffuser output and clean if bubble rate has dropped — a common silent cause of stalled growth.

4. CO2 & lighting basics

More light without more CO2 is the most common way to trigger algae, not better growth. The two need to scale together.

  • Low-tech (no CO2): keep light modest and short (6–7 hrs) and choose low-light plants — anubias, java fern, crypts, most mosses.
  • High-tech (pressurized CO2): turn CO2 on 1 hour before lights, off 1 hour before lights out, target a stable drop checker color rather than chasing a bubble count.
  • Photoperiod: 6–8 hours is standard for planted tanks; more light does not mean more growth if CO2 and nutrients haven't scaled with it.
If you're seeing algae, not better growth: the light is very likely ahead of your CO2/nutrient supply, not "too much light" on its own.

Frequently Asked Questions

What order should you aquascape a tank in?

Hardscape first, placed dry on a table before any substrate or water. Then slope the substrate back to front, plant back-to-front and tall-to-short, and fill the tank slowly over a plate or bag. CO2 and the trim schedule start once everything is in and stable.

How often should you trim an aquascape?

Every one to two weeks for established stem plants, cut back to just above the previous trim line. Letting stems reach the surface and flop is the most common maintenance mistake — it's harder to recover a layout than to keep trimming it on schedule.

Why is my planted tank growing algae instead of plants?

Almost always because the light is running ahead of CO2 and nutrients, not because there's simply too much light. Scale the two together: more intense or longer light needs more CO2 and ferts to match it, or algae fills the gap instead of plants.

Do I need CO2 to aquascape?

No. Low-tech tanks without CO2 can still aquascape well with modest, shorter light (6–7 hours) and undemanding plants like anubias, java fern, crypts and most mosses. Pressurized CO2 opens up faster-growing carpets and stem plants but raises the maintenance and failure-mode list.

Why won't my carpet plant fill in?

Likely planted too densely to begin with. Carpet species like hairgrass, Monte Carlo and glosso fill in faster from many small, spaced-out clumps than from one large dense mat — each clump has room to spread rather than compete with its neighbors. If it's still slow to establish, the dry start method grows it emersed for a few weeks first so roots are already in place before the tank is flooded.
