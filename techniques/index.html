Techniques

Style tells you what you're building. This is how.

Four things determine whether a layout works: what goes in first, how it's planted, how it's maintained, and whether the plants can actually grow under your light and CO2. In that order.

Aquascaping technique comes down to four things, always in this order: hardscape placed dry before anything else, planting back-to-front and tall-to-short, a weekly trim and maintenance routine, and CO2 scaled to match your lighting. Skip the order, or let CO2 lag behind light, and a layout either never comes together or declines within a few months.

Hardscape materials: stone & wood types

Before any of the four steps below, you're choosing what to build with — and that choice has consequences the placement steps don't cover. The two things that matter most: whether a stone is chemically inert or reactive, and whether a piece of wood is going to float and stain your water before it settles down.

  • Seiryu stone — blue-grey, sharp-edged, veined with white quartz. The default choice for Iwagumi, but it's limestone: calcium carbonate dissolves slowly into the water and raises pH and KH over months, not something you want in a soft-water or shrimp-heavy tank.
  • Dragon stone (Ohko) — tan and honeycombed with small pits. Chemically inert, so it won't drift your water chemistry, and the pitted surface gives moss and rhizome plants an easy foothold — a common pick for jungle and nature-style layouts.
  • Lava rock — black or reddish, light, and riddled with pores. Inert and inexpensive, often used as a hidden filler layer under showier stone, or broken up as extra biological filtration media since bacteria colonize its surface area readily.
  • Spiderwood and other true driftwood (manzanita, redmoor) — these float until fully waterlogged, which can take days to weeks, and leach tannins that tint the water tea-brown in the meantime. Soak or weigh it down before planting around it, and expect the tint to fade over several water changes rather than all at once.
Quick chemistry check: a drop of vinegar that fizzes on a stone's surface means it contains carbonate and will raise hardness over time — useful to know before it's cemented into a finished layout. Inert stone (dragon stone, lava rock, most slate) won't fizz.

1. Hardscape placement

Hardscape goes in dry, before substrate is finalized and long before any water. Lay every stone or branch out on a table first — you should be able to walk away and come back without wanting to change anything before it goes in the tank.

  1. Establish a focal point off-center. Use the rule of thirds — never dead-center, never perfectly symmetrical unless the style calls for it (Dutch has no hardscape at all).
  2. Photograph the dry layout from your real viewing angle. A photo flattens the arrangement the way the front glass eventually will, which makes uneven stone spacing or a gap you missed standing over an open tank much easier to spot than it is in person.
  3. Work with one material's natural lines. Mixing stone types or wood species that don't share a grain, color, or erosion pattern is the single most common beginner tell.
  4. Bury a third of what you think you need. Stones and roots that look "placed" usually aren't buried enough — nature doesn't leave things sitting on top of the substrate.
  5. Slope substrate back to front. A rear-high slope (using aquasoil or a substrate support) fakes depth and gives planting room to layer.

2. Planting order

Plant back to front, tall to short, so you're never reaching over something you just placed. Rhizome plants (anubias, bucephalandra, java fern) get tied or glued to hardscape — never buried, or the rhizome rots.

  1. Background stems and tall grasses first.
  2. Midground rhizomes and ferns tied to hardscape.
  3. Foreground carpet or low-growing species last, closest to the glass.
  4. Fill water slowly over a plate or plastic bag laid on the substrate so the flow doesn't uproot fresh plantings.
Carpet plants specifically (hairgrass, Monte Carlo, glosso) do better planted in small clumps with gaps than one dense mat — they fill in faster from many small starts than one struggling large one. For a carpet that's slow to take even planted this way, growing it emersed first with the dry start method lets roots establish before the tank is ever flooded.
Wabi-kusa uses the same emersed-growth principle, but built outside the tank entirely: aquasoil and cuttings wrapped into a mesh-held ball and grown on their own until roots bind it together. Drop the finished ball into a flooded tank for an instant established look, or keep it as its own small above-water display.
Attaching epiphytes (anubias, bucephalandra, java fern, moss) comes down to thread/fishing line or cyanoacrylate super glue gel — skip epoxy and rubber bands. Thread needs no cure time and is forgiving on uneven wood, but has to be cut away once roots grip, usually several weeks in. Glue gel sets in under a minute and disappears as new growth covers it, but keep it off the rhizome itself and the growing tip, or that tissue rots instead of taking hold.

3. Trim & maintenance schedule

A layout that peaks once and declines wasn't maintained — it was assembled. Weekly maintenance is what separates a photo-op from an aquascape that matures.

  • Weekly: water change (30–50%), glass cleaning, dose ferts per your plant load.
  • Every 1–2 weeks: trim stem plants back to just above the previous trim line — never let them reach the surface and flop.
  • Don't toss the trimmings: strip the lower leaves off each cutting, snip the bare stem to a clean end, and replant 2–3 per hole in whatever gap the trim opened up — the cheapest way to thicken a stand instead of buying more plants.
  • As needed: thin carpet plants that are overgrowing their intended footprint; remove any melting or decaying leaves immediately, they foul water fast.
  • Monthly: check CO2 diffuser output and clean if bubble rate has dropped — a common silent cause of stalled growth.
  • Every 2–3 months: rinse filter media in old tank water, not tap water — chlorine and the temperature swing can wipe out the bacteria colony the media is holding.
  • Between water changes, open-top tanks: top off evaporation with RO/DI or distilled water, not tap — tap water left behind as water evaporates concentrates minerals and nutrients instead of diluting them.

4. CO2, lighting & nutrients

More light without more CO2 is the most common way to trigger algae, not better growth. The two need to scale together — and both need nutrients to back them up.

  • Low-tech (no CO2): keep light modest and short (6–7 hrs) and choose low-light plants — anubias, java fern, crypts, most mosses.
  • High-tech (pressurized CO2): turn CO2 on 1 hour before lights, off 1 hour before lights out, target a stable drop checker color rather than chasing a bubble count.
  • Photoperiod: 6–8 hours is standard for planted tanks; more light does not mean more growth if CO2 and nutrients haven't scaled with it.
  • Direct sunlight: a tank near an uncovered window gets light that swings in intensity and duration with the weather and season, which is much harder to balance against CO2 and nutrients than a timer-controlled LED. A sheer curtain, blinds, or simply moving the tank out of the direct beam heads off algae a fixed light schedule wouldn't have caused.
  • Fertilizing: plants need macronutrients (nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium) in regular amounts and micronutrients (iron and other trace elements) in much smaller ones. A liquid all-in-one feeds both through the water column for stem and carpet plants; root tabs feed heavy-root species like crypts and swords directly through the substrate instead, which matters most under sand or gravel that doesn't already carry nutrients the way aquasoil does.
  • Pearling: a stream of fine oxygen bubbles rising off healthy leaves a few hours into the photoperiod is the clearest sign CO2, light and nutrients are already dialed in — a better gut-check than chasing a bubble count or drop checker color alone.
If you're seeing algae, not better growth: the light is very likely ahead of your CO2/nutrient supply, not "too much light" on its own. Diatoms are common early on and usually fade as the tank matures; cyanobacteria tends to show up in low-flow spots instead; green hair algae follows the same light/CO2 imbalance and responds best to twirling it out by hand rather than cutting light alone.

Frequently Asked Questions

What order should you aquascape a tank in?

Hardscape first, placed dry on a table before any substrate or water. Then slope the substrate back to front, plant back-to-front and tall-to-short, and fill the tank slowly over a plate or bag. CO2 and the trim schedule start once everything is in and stable.

How often should you trim an aquascape?

Every one to two weeks for established stem plants, cut back to just above the previous trim line. Letting stems reach the surface and flop is the most common maintenance mistake — it's harder to recover a layout than to keep trimming it on schedule.

Why is my planted tank growing algae instead of plants?

Almost always because the light is running ahead of CO2 and nutrients, not because there's simply too much light. Scale the two together: more intense or longer light needs more CO2 and ferts to match it, or algae fills the gap instead of plants.

How can you tell if a plant is nutrient deficient?

By which leaves show the problem and how. Nitrogen deficiency yellows the oldest leaves first, starting at the tips, while new growth stays green — a cycled tank with fish usually supplies enough on its own. Iron and other micronutrient deficiencies show up the opposite way: new leaves come in pale or yellow with the veins still dark green, while older leaves look fine. Potassium deficiency shows as small pinholes along leaf edges on older leaves. Rule out a CO2 shortage first, since unstable CO2 can mimic some of the same symptoms, and give a fertilizer change two to three weeks before judging whether it worked.

How long do diatoms last in a new planted tank?

A dusty brown film on glass, substrate, and leaves in the first two to three weeks after setup is almost always diatoms, not a sign anything's wrong. They feed on silicates and excess nutrients while the tank's bacteria and plants are still catching up, and typically fade on their own within a few weeks as the tank matures. Keep up weekly water changes and wipe the glass rather than reaching for an algaecide — it's expected feedback at this stage, not a failure.

Do I need CO2 to aquascape?

No. Low-tech tanks without CO2 can still aquascape well with modest, shorter light (6–7 hours) and undemanding plants like anubias, java fern, crypts and most mosses. Pressurized CO2 opens up faster-growing carpets and stem plants but raises the maintenance and failure-mode list.

How often should I rinse my filter, and what water should I use?

Every two to three months for most planted tanks — more often only if flow visibly drops between water changes. Rinse the media in water you've already taken out of the tank, never tap water: the chlorine and temperature difference can kill off the bacteria colony living on the media, which is what's actually handling your nitrogen cycle.

Why won't my carpet plant fill in?

Likely planted too densely to begin with. Carpet species like hairgrass, Monte Carlo and glosso fill in faster from many small, spaced-out clumps than from one large dense mat — each clump has room to spread rather than compete with its neighbors. If it's still slow to establish, the dry start method grows it emersed for a few weeks first so roots are already in place before the tank is flooded.

Is direct sunlight bad for a planted aquarium?

Not inherently, but it's hard to control. Unlike a timer-driven LED, direct sun changes intensity and duration by the hour and by the season, which makes it difficult to keep CO2 and nutrients matched to the light the way the rest of this guide recommends. Diffusing it with a sheer curtain or blinds, or positioning the tank out of the direct beam, is usually simpler than trying to out-dose an unpredictable light source.

Will Seiryu stone raise my aquarium's pH?

Yes, gradually. Seiryu stone is limestone — it contains calcium carbonate that dissolves slowly into the water column and raises both pH and KH over weeks and months. That's fine if you're keeping it for its look and don't need soft water, but it's the wrong choice for a soft-water or shrimp-focused tank. A drop of vinegar that fizzes on a stone's surface is a quick way to spot this kind of reactive stone before it goes in the tank; inert stone like dragon stone or lava rock won't fizz and won't move your water chemistry.

Why is my new driftwood floating, and why is the water turning brown?

Both are normal and temporary. Wood like spiderwood and manzanita floats until it's fully waterlogged, which can take anywhere from a few days to a few weeks — soaking it beforehand or weighing it down with rocks or a suction mount speeds that up. The brown tint is tannins leaching out of the wood; it isn't harmful, and it fades over several water changes as fresh water dilutes it faster than the wood releases more.

What's the difference between wabi-kusa and the dry start method?

Both grow plants emersed before they ever touch standing water, but the dry start method grows them in place, on the substrate and hardscape of the tank they'll stay in. Wabi-kusa grows them separately, as a self-contained ball of aquasoil and cuttings held together in mesh netting, so it can be moved — dropped into a flooded tank for an instant established look, or displayed on its own above water.
